Every seller should know this, and most do not: you can challenge an appraisal, but appraisers rarely move off a number once they have landed on it.

This was a bungalow tucked into downtown Rock Hill. Three bedrooms, two bathrooms, 1,251 square feet. Small footprint, big character. The owners had already put their own touches in: quartz counters, a detached workshop wired for power, a fenced yard, landscaping kept up over the years. It came to market dialed in, not a project for the next owner.

They had bought it in 2020 for $170,000, intending it to be their forever home. A few years in, forever turned out to mean something different. They wanted smaller and they wanted no maintenance. No yard work, no exterior upkeep. That led them to a single level townhome community where the HOA handles the outside.

## The offers and the appraisal

We listed at $295,000 with professional photography and video, marketed it across Instagram, Facebook and YouTube to reach the people drawn to downtown Rock Hill, and hosted two open houses to turn online interest into offers. Multiple offers came in. We negotiated up to a contract at $293,500, the strongest of the group, 16 days after listing.

Then the appraisal came in under it. At 1,251 square feet, this house sat on the smaller side against the comps the appraiser was working from, updates or not. We submitted our own comparable sales to make the case. The appraiser held firm.

## The advice

The obvious move for a seller who has just watched their neighborhood climb in value is to dig in. Fight harder, or pull the listing and try again with a different buyer, betting on a better number next time. I did not recommend that.

The math already told the story. They bought for $170,000 and were walking away with $284,900 close to six years later, a gain of nearly $115,000. Relisting to chase an extra $8,600 meant weeks of carrying costs, a new appraisal with no guarantee of a better number, and real risk to a deal that had already delivered a life changing outcome. We reduced the price to meet the appraisal and closed.

Sometimes the smartest move on the table is the one that looks, on paper, like leaving money behind. Knowing when to hold firm and when to let a good outcome be a good outcome is worth as much as any negotiating tactic.
