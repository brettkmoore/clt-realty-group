A listing that did not sell the first time is not automatically overpriced by a lot. It can be overpriced by a little, marketed poorly, or unlucky in its timing. Townhomes in this market have had a hard run because of HOA costs and what higher rates do to a payment, and a listing with a failed run behind it is a harder sell still. The obvious playbook for a stale listing is to cut again, hard, and then take the first offer that shows up. I did neither.

This was a two story end-unit townhome with a basement, backing to private trees. The owners had kept it immaculate. Nothing to paint, nothing to fix, nothing to stage. The community had a pool, allowed pets, and sat in the pocket between south Charlotte, Uptown and the airport. It also had something most buyers never think to ask about: covenants that cap rentals, which protects a townhome community from turning investor heavy. That matters when you are deciding whether a townhome will hold its value.

It had already failed once. Ninety-six days with a different agent, listed at $430,000, dropped to $425,000, and it expired anyway. The sellers had moved north for work, bought their next home, and were carrying two mortgages while this one sat.

## What I did

I repriced it once, to $415,000. That was a real adjustment to where the market actually was, and I could show the sellers the closed sales behind it. Then I held that number. I skipped staging and prep because the home did not need them. I ran open houses through the first few weeks, including over the July 4th weekend, because a high visibility community gets walk-in traffic on a holiday.

An offer came in below asking. After 96 days of silence, any offer starts to look good. I did not chase the listing's bad history by negotiating down again. We held at $415,000, and the buyer came up. It closed at full asking price, 20 days after relisting.

## Why holding the number was not as risky as it sounds

Panicking and slashing the price teaches buyers to wait you out. Repricing accurately once, backing it with real exposure, and then standing behind the number is what gets a listing with a history across the finish line at full value. Knowing the market well enough to reprice with confidence, and knowing the buyer well enough to not blink when they test you, is what turns a problem listing into a closed deal.

For two sellers paying two mortgages, twenty days was relief.
